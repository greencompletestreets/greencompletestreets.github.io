Why it matters

Why Green Complete Streets?

Streets are experienced by real people under real conditions. A route may exist on a plan or map but still be difficult to use because it lacks shade, accessible connections, comfortable waiting areas, healthy tree canopy, protection from heat, or reliable maintenance.

Green Complete Streets focus on whether streets actually work for people — not simply whether individual features have been installed.

Streets have to work for people

A successful street should help people reach everyday destinations, travel independently, remain comfortable in changing weather, access transit, walk, bike, roll, use mobility devices, spend time in public space, connect with parks and nature, and use the street across different ages and abilities.

Safety, comfort, accessibility, climate resilience, health, and usability are interconnected. Good streets should support them together.

Comfort, health, and everyday usability

A street is not successful simply because a route exists. A genuinely usable street should be comfortable, understandable, connected, accessible, reliable, maintained, and usable under real environmental conditions.

The design of a street affects whether people can walk, bike, roll, reach transit, visit businesses, access services, spend time outdoors, or participate in community life.

Shade, heat protection, and thermal comfort

Large areas of asphalt and concrete absorb and radiate heat. Lack of shade can make walking, biking, rolling, using mobility devices, waiting for transit, or spending time in public space difficult or unsafe during hot weather.

Shade is a transportation need, an accessibility need, a public-health need, and a climate-resilience need.

Urban forest and tree canopy

A healthy urban forest creates the canopy needed to make streets cooler and more comfortable. Canopy can shade sidewalks, bikeways, transit routes and stops, and public spaces while reducing heat exposure, supporting air quality, helping manage stormwater, protecting pavement, and supporting habitat.

Tree canopy is a long-term outcome requiring space, soil, appropriate tree selection, care, protection, water where necessary, maintenance, and replacement. It should not be measured only by the number of trees planted.

Walking, biking, rolling, and transit access

Transportation infrastructure and environmental design should reinforce one another. Shade, trees, stormwater features, seating, accessible paths, comfortable public space, route continuity, maintenance, and connections to destinations all affect whether walking, biking, rolling, and transit access are genuinely usable.

The emphasis should be on people’s full experience of the route rather than on individual elements in isolation.

Stormwater management and green infrastructure

Conventional paved streets create runoff that can carry pollutants into storm drains and waterways. Green infrastructure can capture runoff, slow water, filter pollutants, reduce surface pooling, support infiltration where appropriate, reduce pressure on drainage systems, protect creeks and the bay, and support trees and planted areas.

Examples include rain gardens, bioswales, bioretention areas, permeable surfaces, healthy soils, planted medians, and stormwater curb extensions.

Biodiversity and ecological function

Streets can contribute to native planting, pollinator support, habitat, wildlife movement, ecological connectivity, soil health, healthier waterways, and connections between parks and other green spaces.

Biodiversity should be integrated into street design rather than treated as ornamental landscaping.

Public space and connection

Streets are some of the largest public spaces in a city. They should connect people to homes, schools, businesses, services, parks, transit, community destinations, and nature.

Streets should not function only as corridors for vehicles. They should also support everyday social life, gathering, rest, connection, and comfort.

Cleaner air and healthier streets

Trees, vegetation, reduced heat exposure, comfortable active transportation, and better public-space conditions can support health. A healthier street environment can make it easier for people to spend time outdoors and use active transportation.

Equity and access

Access to shade, healthy canopy, comfortable public space, safe mobility, clean air, parks, and climate resilience is not evenly distributed. Green Complete Streets should help identify and address those disparities.

Long-term maintenance and stewardship

Green infrastructure only works over time if it is maintained. Planning and funding for tree establishment, irrigation where necessary, pruning, plant care, stormwater-system cleaning, repair, replacement, inspection, and monitoring are essential.

A project should not be considered successful simply because it looked complete on opening day.

Integrated planning and implementation

Transportation, urban forestry, stormwater, biodiversity, accessibility, public space, utilities, and maintenance are often handled by separate teams, funding sources, standards, and schedules. That separation can create fragmented outcomes.

Green Complete Streets require these systems to be coordinated early enough to shape project scope, design, funding, construction, maintenance, and measurement.

Questions a people-first street should answer

  • Can people use the street safely?
  • Can people use it comfortably?
  • Can people use it during hot weather?
  • Can people of different ages and abilities use it?
  • Does it connect people to everyday destinations?
  • Does it support walking, biking, rolling, and transit access?
  • Does it provide meaningful shade and canopy?
  • Does it manage stormwater as part of the street?
  • Does it support biodiversity and ecological function?
  • Can its features be maintained over time?
  • Are the benefits distributed equitably?

What a better approach changes

Green Complete Streets move environmental performance, human comfort, accessibility, and maintenance into the core of street planning. They do not treat these issues as separate programs or late additions.
