Why it matters

Why Green Complete Streets Matter

Climate change is often discussed at a global or regional scale through extreme heat, flooding, drought, and air pollution. But where many of those conditions affect us in daily life is much closer to home, on the streets outside our front doors.

Streets connect people to homes, schools, businesses, parks, transit, and essential services. They are also some of the largest public spaces in our communities and one of the main places where people experience heat, traffic, pollution, shade, trees, water, and public life.

Designed poorly, streets can amplify heat, create runoff, expose people to traffic and pollution, and make walking, biking, rolling, or waiting for transit difficult or unpleasant. Designed well, they can buffer people from those conditions, manage water, support trees and habitat, and create safer, cooler, more comfortable places to move through and spend time in. That basic contrast is central to the way you framed streets in your ATP presentation: as the interface between climate and community.

Infrastructure can exist without working

A sidewalk, bikeway, crossing, or transit stop may exist on a plan or map but still be difficult to use. It may be fully exposed to the sun, blocked by flooding, disconnected from destinations, inaccessible, uncomfortable, or poorly maintained.

A route should not be considered successful simply because it was built or technically remains open. The more important question is whether people can actually use it safely, comfortably, and independently when they need it.

This distinction matters because people experience performance, not only presence. A crossing that disappears under water is not available. A sidewalk that becomes intensely hot in the afternoon may not be reasonably usable for many people. A route that repeatedly fails during rain, heat, or poor maintenance may no longer be something people can rely on, even if it works under ideal conditions.

Streets have to work for people

A successful street should help people reach homes, schools, businesses, services, parks, transit, and other everyday destinations. It should support people walking, biking, rolling, using mobility devices, waiting for transit, resting, and spending time outside.

That requires more than individual facilities. The full route should be connected, accessible, understandable, maintained, and comfortable. A well-designed block does not solve a trip that fails at the next intersection. A shaded segment does not fully address a route if the crossing or transit stop remains exposed. A sidewalk is not genuinely usable if it is obstructed, disconnected, or regularly flooded.

People’s behavior often shows where the street is not meeting their needs. They cross to the shaded side, gather in narrow patches of shade, avoid flooded crossings, move into the roadway, travel at different times, or create informal paths where the designed route is too exposed or indirect. These choices are not random. They are adaptations to the conditions people actually experience.

Heat, shade, and comfort

Large areas of asphalt and concrete absorb heat from the sun and radiate it back into the surrounding space. As a result, a sidewalk, bikeway, crossing, transit stop, or public space may technically remain open while becoming extremely uncomfortable or unsafe to use.

Heat affects whether people can travel, how far they can go, when they can make a trip, and whether they can comfortably wait, rest, or spend time outside. It does not affect everyone equally. Older adults, children, people with disabilities or chronic health conditions, outdoor workers, people carrying groceries, people accompanying children, and people who depend on walking or transit may be especially affected.

Shade can reduce exposure, improve comfort, support access to transit and everyday destinations, and extend the times when people can use a street. It should be considered along the full route, including the places where people walk, bike, cross, wait, and rest.

Shade is therefore not simply an amenity. It is a transportation need, an accessibility need, a public-health need, and a climate-resilience need.

Trees and lasting canopy

Trees are one of the most effective ways to provide shade and improve the street environment. A healthy urban forest can shade sidewalks, bikeways, crossings, transit stops, and public spaces while also supporting stormwater management, habitat, soil health, air quality, and a more comfortable public realm.

Those benefits do not come from planting alone. Trees need enough soil and growing space, appropriate species selection, establishment care, water where necessary, protection, maintenance, monitoring, and replacement. Without those conditions, a project may add trees without ever producing meaningful canopy.

The outcome that matters is not simply the number of trees planted. It is whether those trees survive, grow, provide shade, support ecological function, and benefit the people and places with the greatest need.

Flooding and street usability

Flooding is not only a stormwater or drainage issue. It is also a transportation and accessibility issue. When streets flood, crossings can disappear, bike lanes can become impassable, sidewalks can be blocked, and people may be forced to turn around, detour, or enter traffic.

Regional flood maps are useful for understanding large-scale risk, but they may not show the intersections and blocks that repeatedly pond after ordinary storms. Maintenance workers and community members often know where those everyday failures occur because they see the same drains clog and the same crossings become unusable after each storm.

Green stormwater infrastructure can help streets capture and slow runoff, reduce surface pooling, filter pollutants, and remain usable during a wider range of conditions. When it is integrated with trees, planting, crossings, sidewalks, and public space, it can improve more than drainage alone.

Public space and community life

Streets are not only corridors for moving vehicles. They are also some of the largest public spaces in a city. They connect people to homes, schools, businesses, services, parks, transit, community destinations, and nature. They are places where people wait, meet, rest, gather, and spend time outdoors.

Street design affects whether people feel able to participate in daily and community life. A street that is shaded, connected, accessible, and comfortable can make it easier to reach local businesses, accompany children, visit a park, use transit, attend an event, or maintain social connections. A street that is hostile, exposed, or difficult to navigate can limit those same activities.

Green Complete Streets recognize that movement, comfort, and public life are connected. Streets should not only move people through a place. They should also help people use and connect with the places around them.

Equity and access

Access to shade, healthy canopy, comfortable public space, safe mobility, clean air, and climate resilience is not evenly distributed. Some neighborhoods have mature trees, comfortable sidewalks, frequent transit, and well-maintained public spaces. Others have wide roads, large paved areas, limited shade, poor walking connections, greater pollution exposure, and fewer resources for maintenance or improvement.

Green Complete Streets should help identify and address those disparities through project selection, design, funding, implementation, maintenance, and measurement. The question is not only where features are installed, but who benefits, who remains exposed, and whether those benefits endure.

Equity should not appear only as a general policy statement. It should influence where investments are made, which problems are addressed first, how communities are involved, and how success is evaluated over time.

Streets need to keep working

A project is not successful simply because it looked complete on opening day. Trees may die, stormwater systems may clog, planting may fail, sidewalks and bikeways may become obstructed, and accessible paths may deteriorate.

Long-term care is part of whether the infrastructure works. Planning and funding are needed for tree establishment, irrigation where necessary, pruning, plant care, stormwater-system cleaning, inspection, repair, monitoring, and replacement. Responsibilities should be clear before construction is complete, and long-term operating needs should be reflected in project scope and funding.

Success should be measured not only by miles built, trees planted, or projects completed. It should also be measured by whether people can use the street safely and comfortably, whether trees survive and produce shade, whether stormwater systems function, whether maintenance is funded, and whether the street continues to serve the people it was intended to benefit.

A better measure of success

Green Complete Streets shift attention from what was installed to what the street actually achieves. They bring human comfort, accessibility, environmental performance, and long-term stewardship into the core of street planning rather than treating them as separate programs or late additions.

This does not mean every street has to be perfect or contain every possible feature. It means understanding the conditions people experience, making tradeoffs visible, and designing streets to perform better over time.

A street that only works under ideal conditions is not fully working. A better approach asks whether the street remains safe, usable, comfortable, resilient, and beneficial for people and the environment under the conditions they actually face.
