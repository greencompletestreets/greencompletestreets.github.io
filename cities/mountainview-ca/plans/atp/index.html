Council adopts the 2026 ATP

Mountain View City Council adopted the 2026 Active Transportation Plan on September 22, 2026.

Before adoption, a coalition of local organizations and residents requested amendments addressing design standards, greening, performance measures, walkability, traffic calming, bicycle facilities, and smaller-scale safety improvements.

Staff responded to those requests during the Council meeting, and Council gave direction on several of them.

Vision & guiding principles

What does the ATP envision?

The MVATP is built around a Vision Statement and five Guiding Principles, developed with the ATPAC, TAC and BPAC and approved by City Council. Together they set the standard against which every recommendation in the plan — and every recommendation on this page — is measured.

“Mountain View leads the region with an Active Transportation Plan that defines a series of connected, low-stress, and green corridors that invite active transportation. The corridors include shade, wildlife habitat, and public open spaces. The plan enables the City to intentionally support a culture of walking and biking, enhance biodiversity, and reduce climate change impacts.”

MVATP Vision Statement, Chapter 1

The five guiding principles

  • Safety & Comfort
  • Access & Equity
  • Mobility & Connectivity
  • Sustainability & Biodiversity
  • Innovation & Action-Orientation

The first four also organize the project scoring criteria below (plus a separate Feasibility category that isn’t tied to a guiding principle). Same colors, same categories, everywhere on this page.

How a project gets chosen

From network to priority projects

The MVATP began with a citywide walking, bicycling and rolling network and identified more than 100 potential infrastructure projects. Projects were screened for feasibility and scored using the MVATP Scoring Criteria Framework: 17 criteria worth 100 points across five categories. City staff then reviewed the 24 highest-scoring projects, advancing 20 for concept development; four others already had plans underway or were substantially within Caltrans right-of-way. Detailed concepts for 16 priority projects are included in Appendix B.

Beyond capital projects

Policies & programs

“Infrastructure changes are not the only ‘tool in the toolbox’ to improve the travel experience for people who walk, roll, and ride in Mountain View.” Table 7 lists 23 policies and programs — aligned with the MVATP vision and guiding principles — that support active transportation alongside capital projects.

Explore the policies and programs by category below. This is the full list from Table 7 (23 actions, unranked).

    • No Right Turn on Red PolicyPilot to prohibit right turns on red at selected signalized intersections to improve safety, comfort and confidence for pedestrians and cyclists.
    • Leading Pedestrian Interval PolicyGive pedestrians a head start entering the crosswalk at intersections evaluated and recommended by staff.
    • Upgrade Pedestrian Signals to Accessible Pedestrian Signals (APS)Provide non-visual information — auditory tones, messages and/or vibrations — about when to cross the street.
    • Improve Visibility at Intersections (Daylighting)Implement and enforce California’s Daylighting Law (AB 413), prohibiting parking within 20 feet of a crosswalk approach (15 feet with a curb extension) to improve visibility for everyone.
    • Evaluation of Speed Limits and Speed ZonesExamine speed-limit reductions allowable under the California Vehicle Code, informed by post-project or district/zone engineering and traffic surveys.
    • Enforcement of School Zone Speed LimitsPromote driver awareness and caution in areas where youth are gathered.
    • Managing and Enforcing the Transportation System of All Road UsersEnforce space allocated to each mode — no parking or stopping in bicycle lanes, managing sidewalk space — to reduce modal conflict and crash risk.
    • Road Safety EducationRoad safety education for all modes and road users to increase awareness of rules of the road and traveler responsibilities.
    • Update Bicycle Parking StandardsSimplify and add flexibility to municipal code bicycle-parking standards to allow more high-quality bicycle parking.
    • Education and Enforcement on Use of Personal Electric Micromobility DevicesPublic education and safety training, through Safe Routes to School and other outlets, on legal and appropriate e-bike/e-scooter use.
    • Bicycle Friendly Community DesignationPursue the League of American Bicyclists’ designation, opening up resources supporting bicycle infrastructure and policy.
    • Consideration of Pedestrian Connectivity via Paseos in Property DevelopmentNarrow pedestrian-only paths between buildings, alleys or public staircases that provide safe walking and biking connections across neighborhoods.
    • Walk Safe DesignationPursue America Walks’ Walk Safe Community designation and related Community Change grants.
    • Expansion of Safe Routes ProgramsAdd Safe Routes to Parks and Safe Routes to Transit alongside the existing Safe Routes to School program.
    • Social Walks/RidesRegularly scheduled, community-promoted walk or bicycle tours focused on encouragement, training for new users, and road-safety awareness.
    • Updated Design Standards to be Informed by Best PracticesUse best-practice guidance and standards (Caltrans, FHWA, AASHTO, U.S. Access Board, NACTO, ITE) to inform transportation project design for roadway users of all ages and abilities.
    • Transportation Demand Management (TDM)Reduce automobile dependency through policy and programs, supporting infrastructure investments by making walking and biking more feasible. Mountain View’s TDM ordinance took effect in June 2026.
    • Staff Continuing EducationContinuing education on the Vision Zero Action Plan, Local Road Safety Plan, defensive driving, the Safe Systems Approach and safety countermeasures.
    • Greening / Tree Canopy ImprovementsIncorporate greenery and street trees into infrastructural improvements along roadways — serving environmental and planning goals and safer street design alike. See Green Complete Streets below.
    • Maintenance of Active Transportation FacilitiesFund and program regular maintenance — bicycle lane sweeping, sidewalk cleaning and repair — to uphold safety and comfort.
    • Waste Bin Policy and MaintenancePlace waste bins conspicuously throughout Mountain View to promote proper disposal.
    • Evaluation of Infrastructure ProjectsMonitor whether built projects achieve their intended safety and travel-behavior impact, including pre/post counts required by funding programs like the CTC’s Active Transportation Program. See Measuring Progress below.
    • Develop Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to Measure Active Transportation ProgressCity staff develop KPIs to measure and report annual overall progress toward the ATP Guiding Principles. See Measuring Progress below.

From Plan Goals to Real Streets

The ATP contains many of the ingredients of a Green Complete Street. The question is how well these goals are integrated when projects are designed, funded and built.

Mountain View’s Active Transportation Plan goes beyond traditional transportation planning. It addresses safe walking and bicycling, accessibility, trees and shade, biodiversity, stormwater management and equity. Some of these elements are core project-prioritization criteria; others enter through connections to plans like the Biodiversity and Urban Forest Plan (BUFP).

This section reviews how the ATP addresses each of these elements and examines where integration happens — and where there are opportunities to strengthen the connection from plan to implementation.

A Green Complete Street works as a system.

Safe and useful for people, healthy for the environment, and equitable in who benefits.

Streets are more than travel corridors. They are public spaces that move people, support ecosystems and strengthen communities.

A Green Complete Streets reading

What makes a Green Complete Street?

A Green Complete Street integrates people, environment and equity. These elements work together — not in isolation — to create streets that are safe, comfortable, green and useful for everyone in Mountain View.

Safe + Useful
for People

Move, connect and belong.

  • Walking and bicycling networks
  • Safe crossings and intersections
  • All-ages accessibility
  • Connections to transit, parks and services
  • Comfort and a high-quality public realm

Climate + Ecology

Healthy, resilient and regenerative.

  • Shade and urban forestry
  • Biodiversity and habitat
  • Stormwater management
  • Heat resilience
  • Green infrastructure in the right-of-way
+

Equity

Fair access and shared benefits.

  • Who has access and who benefits
  • Geographic and demographic prioritization
  • Investments in underserved communities
  • Inclusive engagement
  • Affordability and anti-displacement considerations

Safe + comfortable · green · equitable

These elements are designed and delivered together — not as separate transportation, forestry, stormwater and equity projects.

Closing the loop

Measuring progress

Table 7 names two distinct kinds of measurement — one project-level, one citywide:

Evaluation of Infrastructure Projects

Monitors whether an individual built project achieved its intended impact on travel behavior and safety for multiple modes. Funding programs such as the CTC’s Active Transportation Program require pre- and post-project counts of walking, bicycling and vehicles.

Develop KPIs to Measure Active Transportation Progress

City staff develop key performance indicators to measure and report annual overall progress toward the ATP Guiding Principles — a citywide measure, distinct from any single project’s evaluation.

The ATP doesn’t yet specify what those KPIs are. What Green Complete Streets thinks should be measured — and how — is planned as a later addition to this section.
