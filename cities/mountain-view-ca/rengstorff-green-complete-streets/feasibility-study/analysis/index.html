Analysis

Reader task: distinguish categories of decision

Technical feasibility vs. evaluation constraints

Four different kinds of statement can get conflated when a concept like the road diet is described as "not advanced." Distinguishing them matters for interpreting the record.

Physical / engineering feasibility

Whether a design can physically be built and operated safely, independent of anyone's preference for it.

Traffic-operational impact

A measured or modeled effect, such as intersection LOS or travel time, that results from a design choice.

Evaluation judgment

A determination that a measured impact is or is not acceptable, based on some criterion or threshold.

Policy choice

A decision about what the corridor should prioritize, made by someone with the authority to make that tradeoff.

Analysis

Reader task: understand why the missing link matters

What role did LOS play?

LOS is documented as a traffic-operational impact: the study measured it, at two intersections, for one concept. What is not yet documented is whether LOS also functioned as an evaluation judgment — a threshold a concept had to clear to keep advancing.

Why the criteria/threshold connection matters

The approved SOW's 10 original evaluation criteria do not name LOS, vehicle delay, or capacity/throughput (see Evaluation criteria on the Screening page). If LOS became a screening threshold, that would have to have happened through the SOW's own allowance for the criteria to be revised based on TAC and community feedback — which is exactly the link the still-missing Screening Criteria Memorandum and TAC records would show. Until then, "the road diet produced worse LOS" and "the road diet was screened out because of LOS" are not the same documented statement.

Reader task: understand a downstream consequence

Four lanes as a design constraint

Once four general-purpose travel lanes are treated as fixed, the following elements compete for whatever corridor width remains:

  • Protected bikeways
  • Buffers
  • Sidewalks
  • Trees
  • Green stormwater infrastructure
  • Medians
  • Parking
  • Transit treatments
The analytical question

Are these elements competing because the corridor is physically unable to accommodate them together, or because vehicle lane allocation was removed from the design variables?

Parking and bikeway separation design requirements

External California design guidance — not Rengstorff-specific proposed dimensions

Current Caltrans DIB 89-02 guidance for separation between bikeways, parking, and physical buffers:

Condition Separation requirement / guidance

Raised island with accessible parking5’ minimum

Caltrans DIB 89-02 specifies a 3-foot minimum marked buffer between on-street parking and a separated bikeway, increasing to 5 feet at accessible parking. Without parking, several forms of physical separation use a 2-foot minimum and 3-foot preferred width.

Source: Caltrans Design Information Bulletin 89-02, Class IV Bikeway Guidance, Section 3.3. A verified public URL for this citation has not yet been added to this repository — see the research tracker.
