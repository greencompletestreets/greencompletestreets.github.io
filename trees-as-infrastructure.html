Trees as Infrastructure

A documented chronology of the ideas, public comments, letters, and presentations through which April Webster developed a systems-based approach to urban forestry, transportation, climate resilience, and municipal asset management.

The central idea

Urban trees are often treated as landscaping: desirable, but secondary to the “real” infrastructure of streets, drainage, utilities, and buildings. This work develops a different model.

Trees perform public functions, interact with transportation and stormwater systems, require long-term maintenance and replacement, and can be measured through outcomes such as shade, canopy, thermal comfort, stormwater performance, and public use.

If trees consistently perform public functions, require lifecycle management, influence other municipal systems, and need standards, funding, metrics, and performance tracking, then they should be represented as municipal infrastructure assets.

Development timeline

The record shows a coherent progression from nature as civic infrastructure to a performance-based, lifecycle, and asset-management framework.

November 11, 2025

Nature as Core Infrastructure

“Recognizing the urban forest as core civic infrastructure means managing and funding it on par with streets and stormwater systems.”
“The City should formally classify trees and green infrastructure as capital assets within the CIP and asset-management systems.”

This letter established the core proposition: the urban forest should be funded, governed, inventoried, and maintained as civic infrastructure. It also called for soil-volume and condition tracking, recurring capital funding, lifecycle management, cross-department coordination, and integration with transportation and stormwater projects.

March 11, 2026

Corridor trees as long-term public assets

“Corridor trees function as long-term public assets that provide shade, stormwater management, and heat mitigation.”
“Many municipalities now treat urban trees as infrastructure assets within their asset management systems because they provide measurable environmental and public health benefits.”

The Foothill Expressway comments applied the framework to a transportation corridor. They asked that tree removal, replacement, establishment, and lifecycle impacts be included in project cost comparisons, and identified interactions among canopy, pavement deterioration, thermal comfort, and facility usability.

March 12, 2026

Performance-based street design

“A bike lane that floods every winter isn't infrastructure. A sidewalk that bakes at 110 degrees by noon isn't usable, and a street that only works for part of the day isn't really working.”
“Treating shade, temperature exposure, and stormwater management as core performance metrics rather than nice-to-haves.”

This framing moved the argument from amenities to infrastructure performance. Shade, temperature, and stormwater were treated as operational characteristics that determine whether a walking or bicycling facility remains usable.

March 28, 2026

Canopy as functional transportation infrastructure

“Canopy isn't just aesthetic—it really affects comfort, usability, and climate safety for people walking and biking, and can directly influence how many people actually use a facility.”

This email connected canopy directly to transportation performance and demand, and framed trees, walking, biking, and vehicle movement as coordinated uses of the same constrained public right-of-way.

March 29, 2026

Embedding tree outcomes in municipal standards

“Objective Design Standards set the requirements … while Standard Details determine how things actually get built on the ground.”

The message called for objective requirements for preserving existing trees and providing sufficient soil volume, while emphasizing that municipal engineering details govern placement, spacing, soil systems, and ultimately what gets delivered.

April 13, 2026

Default engineering templates shape canopy outcomes

“Standard Details are the default engineering templates used for street design … Because they're the starting point for most projects, they end up shaping outcomes like bike facilities, street trees, and small open space opportunities.”

This connected citywide outcomes to the rules and templates embedded in public works practice. Updating engineering defaults was identified as a direct way to align policy with implementation.

April 15, 2026

The municipal systems gap

“We have well-defined metrics and dedicated programs for things like pavement condition (PCI), but we don't have equivalent, consistently tracked measures for tree canopy, biodiversity, active transportation, or parks.”
“Some assets, like roadway infrastructure, are treated as core systems, with clear standards, dedicated funding, and regular performance tracking, while others—like tree canopy, active transportation infrastructure, biodiversity, and parks—aren't structured the same way.”

This is the clearest diagnosis of the information and governance problem: municipalities privilege assets represented through standards, funding programs, inventories, and performance measures. Other public systems remain easier to defer because they are not modeled or managed in the same way.

April 28, 2026

From vision to measurable delivery

“Stronger implementation language helps translate adopted goals into measurable and consistent outcomes.”

The City Council presentation brought the framework together through implementation, accountability, annual reporting, standards, quantified canopy targets, and integration of green infrastructure into sidewalks, bikeways, and public-realm delivery.

Framework principles

The chronology supports a model of the urban forest as a distributed infrastructure system—not a collection of isolated landscape objects.

Public function

Trees provide shade, cooling, stormwater management, habitat, public-health protection, and more usable transportation facilities.

Lifecycle management

Trees require planning, establishment, irrigation, inspection, maintenance, renewal, and replacement over long time horizons.

System interaction

Trees influence pavement life, drainage, visibility, travel behavior, thermal comfort, biodiversity, and public-space quality.

Standards and defaults

Objective standards, engineering details, soil specifications, and right-of-way allocation determine whether canopy survives project delivery.

Measurement and accountability

Canopy, shade, condition, soil volume, establishment success, equity, and corridor performance can be tracked as municipal service outcomes.

Funding and governance

Durable outcomes require capital classification, recurring maintenance funding, clear ownership, public reporting, and cross-department coordination.

Documentary record

This page documents the development of the framework through contemporaneous emails, public letters, advocacy materials, and presentations produced between November 2025 and April 2026.

Several messages were sent directly to, copied to, or blind-copied to Jean-Paul Renaud of Canopy, and he responded positively to the November 2025 letter. That establishes prior authorship and access to the ideas. It does not, by itself, establish deliberate copying or intent.

The purpose of this page is to preserve the chronology clearly, accurately, and in the original author's own words.
