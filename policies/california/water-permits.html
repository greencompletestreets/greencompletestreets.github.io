Municipal Regional Stormwater NPDES 3.0

MRP 3.0 Background and Key Changes

MRP permits operate on five-year cycles. Our current permit, MRP 3.0, runs from July 2022 through June 2027, and it is significantly more stringent than previous versions.

Earlier permits relied heavily on stormwater management plans and included many “should” statements, making compliance difficult to measure.

MRP 3.0 shifts from “should” to “shall”, moving expectations directly into the permit and creating clear, enforceable requirements. It also introduces:

  • the first numeric GSI retrofit mandate
  • lowers thresholds so more projects must treat stormwater, and
  • expands regulation of right-of-way projects.

MRP 4.0 will be released in 2027. Any unmet retrofit numeric requirements from MRP 3.0 will likely be met with a fine, and will roll forward into the MRP 4.0 permit cycle that is likely to be even stricter.

What is MRP 3.0 C.3?

The Municipal Regional Stormwater Permit (Order R2-2022-0018) governs how cities manage stormwater to protect creeks and the Bay. Provision C.3 sets post-construction stormwater requirements for new development, redevelopment, and (new in this cycle) retrofits of existing impervious surfaces.

Key goals

Reduce polluted runoff, treat water near its source, retrofit the existing built environment, and integrate Green Stormwater Infrastructure (GSI) wherever feasible.

Core requirements

  • Impervious thresholds: Most projects that create or replace ≥ 5,000 sq ft of impervious surface must implement LID/GSI; single-family modifications trigger at 10,000 sq ft. Many right-of-way paving/reconstruction projects now fall under C.3.
  • Treatment measures: Approved systems include bioretention, stormwater planters, infiltration, permeable pavement, and rainwater harvesting. They must be sized using volume- or flow-based criteria to treat a defined portion of runoff.
  • Hydromodification (C.3.g): Projects creating/replacing ≥ 1 acre of impervious surface in HM-susceptible areas must manage increased flows and durations.
  • Retrofit requirement (C.3.j): Cities must retrofit existing hardscape with GSI. Mountain View’s numeric target is 4.96 acres of impervious-to-pervious retrofit. If unmet, obligations roll forward and future permit cycles will be stricter.

Why Streets Matter

Streets and paved public rights-of-way often make up up to 80% of a city’s land area. Because they are mostly impervious, they represent the largest realistic opportunity to meet Mountain View’s MRP 3.0 retrofit obligations.

Three City Plans in Flight — and How They Align With C.3

  • Active Transportation Plan: corridor redesigns offer opportunities to integrate stormwater planters, permeable bikeways, and GSI tree systems; these support ATP goals for shade, cooling, and lower mean radiant temperature.
  • Biodiversity & Urban Forest Plan: proposes canopy expansion in the public right-of-way; converting standard tree wells into GSI tree wells achieves canopy, cooling, soil health, and stormwater targets simultaneously.
  • Parks & Recreation Strategic Plan: park edges, trails, plazas, and recreational spaces can incorporate permeable surfaces and bioretention; this supports green space access, resilience, and adds and adds important non-street GSI retrofit opportunities that count toward Mountain View’s MRP 3.0 numeric requirement.

Opportunities for Cross-Department Coordination

Departments: Public Works, Transportation Planning, Fire/Environmental Safety (MRP 3.0), Parks, Community Services (Urban Forest), and the Sustainability and Resiliency

  • Leverage capital projects: Integrate GSI into planned street resurfacing, sidewalk reconstruction, bike lanes, and park upgrades so stormwater treatment, mobility improvements, and livability enhancements occur together.
  • Align canopy and GSI goals: Design tree plantings with adequate soil volumes and engineered flow paths so canopy expansion also delivers stormwater treatment, cooling, shade, walkability, and comfort.
  • Use equity and climate-risk data: Prioritize GSI in heat-vulnerable, low-canopy neighborhoods using Healthy Places Index, canopy mapping, and heat-exposure data to support climate resilience and equitable investment.
  • Design multi-benefit corridors: Combine water-quality treatment, protected bikeways, traffic safety, cooling shade, flood mitigation, habitat, and overall livability within the same project footprint.
  • Create non-street GSI opportunities: Use parks, plazas, trails, and public facilities to add permeable surfaces, bioretention, and GSI tree systems, contributing additional retrofit acreage beyond the street network.
  • Reduce long-term costs: Bundle GSI with scheduled City projects rather than retrofitting later, minimizing construction impacts, reducing overall cost, and accelerating progress toward Mountain View’s 4.96-acre GSI target.

Green Infrastructure vs. Green Stormwater Infrastructure

Green Infrastructure (GI) refers to trees, landscaping, habitat, and nature-based improvements that support cooling, shade, biodiversity, and placemaking.

Green Stormwater Infrastructure (GSI) is a subset of GI with specific engineering requirements to accept, filter, infiltrate, or detain street runoff before it enters the storm drain and ultimately the Bay. Only GSI counts toward MRP 3.0 compliance.

Examples: Trees & Planters

  • Regular street tree: limited soil volume; no engineered flow path; offers shade and habitat but does not treat stormwater and does not count toward MRP 3.0.
  • GSI street tree: includes soil cells or structural soil, curb-cut inlets to capture runoff, engineered soil media, underdrain, and overflow; does count toward the 4.96-acre target.
  • Regular planter (e.g., California Street): decorative, shallow, disconnected from street runoff; provides aesthetic and cooling benefits but not stormwater treatment.
  • Stormwater planter: deeper excavation, engineered soil and gravel layers, curb inlets for runoff, underdrain and overflow structures; provides pollutant removal and runoff reduction and qualifies as GSI.

Cost reality: GSI systems typically cost 3–5× more than standard landscaping due to excavation, utilities coordination, engineered media, and drainage infrastructure.
