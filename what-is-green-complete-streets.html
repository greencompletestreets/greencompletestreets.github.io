What Are Green Complete Streets?

Green Complete Streets treat streets as integrated public infrastructure. They combine safe and accessible transportation with urban forestry, shade, stormwater management, biodiversity, public space, and climate resilience.

They are designed around how people actually experience and use streets — not only how vehicles move through them.

Why “Complete” is intentional

A street is not truly green if it includes landscaping but remains unsafe, inaccessible, uncomfortable, disconnected, or difficult to use.

A street is not complete if it improves mobility while ignoring heat, shade, flooding, biodiversity, ecological conditions, public space, accessibility, or long-term maintenance.

Green Complete Streets bring transportation, environmental systems, public space, accessibility, and long-term stewardship together from the beginning.

A people-first street system

Green Complete Streets begin with people’s daily experience. They are meant to support people who are walking, biking, rolling, using mobility devices, reaching transit, driving, living along the street, working nearby, visiting businesses and services, and gathering in public space.

Usability involves more than the existence of infrastructure. Streets should be understandable, connected, accessible, comfortable, reliable, and usable in real conditions.

The parts of a Green Complete Street

Walking, rolling, and accessibility

Sidewalks, crossings, paths, curb ramps, waiting areas, and public spaces should support people with different ages, abilities, and mobility needs.

Continuity, accessibility, comfort, independent mobility, and actual usability matter more than the presence of a painted line or a single curb ramp.

Bicycling and micromobility

Bicycle and micromobility facilities should be safe, connected, understandable, comfortable, and usable by people with a range of ages and abilities.

They should be coordinated with trees, shade, transit, public space, stormwater features, accessibility, and maintenance.

Transit access and comfort

Transit access includes the complete trip to and from a stop. Walking routes, rolling routes, bicycle connections, accessibility, shade, heat exposure, seating, waiting conditions, route continuity, and nearby destinations all affect whether transit is genuinely usable.

Urban forest and tree canopy

Trees are infrastructure. Healthy and continuous tree canopy can provide shade, reduce heat exposure, support air quality, help manage stormwater, support biodiversity, protect pavement, and make active travel more usable.

Canopy depends on sufficient soil volume, healthy soils, appropriate species selection, establishment care, water where necessary, protection from damage, pruning, maintenance, monitoring, and eventual replacement.

Shade and thermal comfort

Shade is a functional part of transportation, accessibility, and public-space design. A technically connected route may still be difficult or unsafe to use during hot weather if it lacks shade or places to rest.

Thermal comfort should be considered along sidewalks, bikeways, transit routes, transit stops, crossings, public spaces, and routes to everyday destinations.

Green stormwater infrastructure

Rain gardens, bioswales, bioretention areas, permeable surfaces, healthy soils, planted medians, stormwater curb extensions, and other planted drainage systems can capture, slow, filter, store, and manage runoff.

These systems work best when they are integrated into the street rather than treated as unrelated environmental projects.

Biodiversity and ecological function

Streets can support native planting, pollinators, habitat, wildlife movement, ecological connectivity, soil health, healthier waterways, and connections between parks and other green spaces.

Planting should provide ecological function rather than being treated only as decoration.

Public space and human comfort

Streets are among the largest public spaces in a city. They should connect people to homes, schools, parks, businesses, services, transit, community destinations, and nature.

Streets should feel comfortable, welcoming, legible, useful, and inviting to spend time in.

Maintenance and long-term stewardship

Trees, planting, permeable pavement, stormwater systems, accessibility features, bikeways, transit facilities, and public spaces only work when maintenance responsibilities and funding are established.

Planning should include establishment, inspection, cleaning, repair, pruning, replacement, monitoring, and long-term responsibility from the start.

More than landscaping

Green Complete Streets are not conventional Complete Streets with trees or rain gardens added after the main transportation design is already complete.

They require transportation, accessibility, urban forestry, shade, stormwater, biodiversity, public space, utilities, operations, and maintenance to be considered together early enough to influence the project.

Greenery should not be treated as decoration or an optional enhancement added only when space and funding remain.

Core principles

People-first: Start with how streets are experienced by people.

Safe and accessible: Make streets usable for all ages and abilities.

Designed for all ages and abilities: Support walking, biking, rolling, transit access, and public space.

Climate-resilient: Consider heat, flooding, and long-term environmental performance.

Integrated across systems: Align transportation, trees, stormwater, biodiversity, and maintenance.

Evidence-based: Use measures that reflect real use and outcomes.

Practical and maintainable: Plan for long-term upkeep from the start.

Context-sensitive: Fit the street to the neighborhood, ecosystem, and local climate.

Full lifecycle: Design for installation, operation, maintenance, and eventual replacement.

Measured by outcomes: Focus on whether the street works in practice, not only on what was installed.

How success should be measured

Success should not be measured only by counting installed features. It should be evaluated by whether people can use the street safely and comfortably, whether accessibility and continuity are real, and whether everyday destinations are easier to reach.

Other measures should include shade along actual walking, biking, rolling, and transit routes; thermal comfort; tree survival and canopy growth; stormwater performance; biodiversity and habitat value; network connectivity; maintenance condition; and equitable distribution of benefits.
