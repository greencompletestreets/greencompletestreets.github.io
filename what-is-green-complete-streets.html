What Are Green Complete Streets?

What Are Green Complete Streets?

Green Complete Streets are streets planned, designed, built, and maintained as connected transportation, environmental, and public-space systems. They build on Complete Streets by asking not only whether a street provides for people walking, biking, rolling, using mobility devices, taking transit, and driving, but whether the street actually works for people under the conditions they experience.

A street is more than pavement and more than a way to get from one place to another. It includes the trees and shade above, the soils and stormwater systems below, the sidewalks, bikeways, crossings, and transit facilities people use, the buildings and utilities around it, and the public spaces where people wait, rest, gather, and spend time. Green Complete Streets bring those parts together so the street functions as a whole rather than as a collection of separate projects and requirements.

Building on Complete Streets

Complete Streets helped move transportation planning away from an almost exclusive focus on driving and toward the needs of people of different ages and abilities. They established that streets should support walking, biking, rolling, transit, mobility devices, and other ways of traveling, and that preventing people from being killed or seriously injured must remain the foundation of street design.

Green Complete Streets continue that evolution by recognizing that crash risk is not the only condition that affects whether a street works. People also experience heat, flooding, air pollution, missing shade, poor maintenance, inaccessible connections, and other environmental conditions as they move through a street. A bikeway may be protected from traffic but still be difficult to use during extreme heat. A crossing may meet design standards but become unusable when it floods. A transit stop may exist without providing shade, seating, or a comfortable way to reach it. Green Complete Streets treat those conditions as part of transportation design rather than as separate environmental concerns or optional improvements.

Streets as urban ecosystems

Green Complete Streets treat the street as an urban ecosystem. The trees above, the water and soils below, the transportation infrastructure at the surface, and the people moving through and spending time in the space all affect how the street functions.

These systems are often planned separately even though they occupy the same space and directly affect one another. Utility placement can determine whether trees have enough room to grow. Roadway geometry can determine whether there is space for canopy, planting, or stormwater features. Drainage decisions can affect sidewalks, crossings, and accessible paths. Maintenance decisions determine whether any of those features continue to work over time.

When these parts are planned together, streets can move people safely while also reducing heat exposure, managing water, supporting trees and habitat, and creating more comfortable public space. When they are handled separately, a project may include all of the expected elements and still fail to work well as a whole.

Designing for real conditions

Green Complete Streets focus on how streets function under real conditions, not only on whether infrastructure is technically present. A route may exist on a plan or map but still be difficult to use because it is exposed to heat, blocked by flooding, disconnected from destinations, inaccessible, uncomfortable, or poorly maintained.

People experience the full trip rather than the boundaries between departments, funding programs, or individual projects. A comfortable block does not solve a route that becomes inaccessible at the next intersection. A shaded sidewalk does not help as much if the crossing or transit stop remains fully exposed. A new bikeway does not function as part of a useful network if it repeatedly floods or ends before people reach their destination.

The goal is not simply to install more infrastructure. It is to create streets that people can use safely, comfortably, and independently across different ages, abilities, weather conditions, and trip purposes.

Trees, shade, and water as infrastructure

Trees and vegetation are functional parts of street infrastructure. Healthy canopy can shade sidewalks, bikeways, crossings, transit stops, and public spaces while also supporting stormwater management, habitat, soil health, air quality, and the overall comfort of the street.

But planting trees is not the same as creating canopy. Trees need adequate space and soil, appropriate species selection, establishment care, water where necessary, protection, pruning, monitoring, and eventual replacement. Those needs must be incorporated into street design, project funding, and long-term maintenance. If trees are considered only after lanes, parking, utilities, drainage, and other features have already been fixed, they may be confined to leftover spaces that cannot support mature canopy.

Green stormwater infrastructure is part of the same street system. Rain gardens, bioswales, bioretention areas, permeable surfaces, planted medians, and stormwater curb extensions can slow and filter runoff, reduce surface pooling, support trees, and reduce pressure on conventional drainage systems. When these features are designed as part of the street, they can also reduce paved area, improve crossings and public space, and support habitat and ecological connections.

The value comes from designing these functions together. A stormwater feature should not be treated only as a drainage requirement, and a tree should not be treated only as landscaping. Both affect how the street performs for people and the environment.

Planning and maintaining the whole system

Green Complete Streets require transportation, urban forestry, stormwater, accessibility, utilities, public space, and maintenance teams to coordinate early enough to influence the project. Coordination should shape project scope, alternatives, design, funding, construction, maintenance, and measurement rather than occur only after the main decisions have already been made.

The purpose of coordination is not coordination for its own sake. It is to preserve space for trees and green infrastructure, avoid conflicts, use public funding more effectively, clarify responsibility, and create streets that provide several lasting benefits at once.

That work continues after construction. Trees need establishment care, pruning, protection, monitoring, and replacement. Planting and stormwater systems need cleaning, inspection, and repair. Sidewalks, bikeways, crossings, seating, and accessible paths need to remain clear and functional. Maintenance responsibilities and long-term costs should be understood before a project is built, not worked out only after the infrastructure begins to fail.

Green Complete Streets are not one standard design or a checklist of features every street must contain. They are an approach to planning and managing streets as connected systems, with the aim of creating safer, more comfortable, more resilient, and more useful streets that continue to work over time.
